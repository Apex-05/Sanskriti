About Sanskriti

Preserving India's living traditions through people, memory, and shared stories.

What is Sanskriti?

```

Sanskriti is a community-driven cultural archive of India. Unlike museums or history books that record monuments and rulers, Sanskriti documents everyday life — festivals, rituals, local food, oral traditions, crafts, songs, and practices that exist within families and communities.

Culture is not only what is carved in stone. It is what is remembered, practiced, cooked, sung, and celebrated. Many of these traditions are disappearing as generations change and urban life replaces local knowledge.

Sanskriti gives every individual the power to record their heritage directly on a shared map of India — turning personal memory into collective history.

```

Why Sanskriti Matters

```

Every year, India loses pieces of its cultural memory. Traditional recipes disappear when grandparents pass away. Folk songs are forgotten. Dialects fade. Handicrafts stop being practiced because they are no longer documented or valued.

Most heritage preservation focuses on monuments — forts, temples, and buildings. But the true heritage of a country lives inside its people, not its architecture.

If ordinary traditions are not recorded now, they may never be recorded. Sanskriti exists to ensure that everyday culture is preserved before it is lost.

```
```

Our Mission

To create a public, people-owned digital archive where anyone can document and share the cultural practices of their region.

  • Preserve local traditions and oral knowledge
  • Encourage inter-regional cultural understanding
  • Make heritage accessible to younger generations
  • Record culture before it disappears

Our Vision

To build the largest living cultural map of India — a place where future generations can explore how people actually lived, celebrated, cooked, worshipped, and expressed themselves.

Sanskriti aims to become a long-term historical resource, not just a website — a memory of India created by Indians.

```

A Community Archive

```

Sanskriti is not owned by historians or institutions. It is created by students, families, travelers, artists, and local communities.

Each post becomes a cultural record — a small but meaningful piece of history contributed by a real person. Over time, these individual memories form a collective heritage.
```
